How the score is built · Updated August 2026

A number, and everything it doesn't know

Every company in the deck carries a score out of 100. Here is exactly how it is calculated, what data feeds it, and the list of things it cannot see. The last part is longer than you might expect.

The score is a weighted blend of five factors, each read from figures the company filed with the SEC or from its current market price. It is arithmetic, not judgement. Nothing about it is predictive, and no part of it has been tuned against how these stocks subsequently performed. It is a screen: a way of asking whether the reported numbers look cheap, whether they are growing, and whether much of the revenue survives to the bottom line.

A company scoring 76 is not a better investment than one scoring 41. It is a company whose filings score higher against these particular thresholds, which is a much narrower claim.

Where a company has not reported the figures a factor needs, that factor is dropped and the remaining weights are rescaled. A score built from three factors is shown the same way as one built from five, which is a real weakness and is listed again below.

How a factor becomes a number

Each input is mapped onto a 0–100 curve through fixed anchor points, with straight-line interpolation between them. The anchors were chosen to sit near long-run market norms, not fitted to any historical return. Value, for instance, reads the price-to-earnings ratio like this:

Price-to-book and price-to-sales are mapped the same way and averaged in, with earnings counted twice because it is the figure most people are actually pricing. Growth reads year-over-year revenue, earnings per share, and the five-year revenue trend, with revenue weighted double because it is far less noisy than EPS. The other three factors work identically.

The five results are multiplied by their weights and summed. Above 70 the card says screens strongly; below 33, screens badly. The bands in between are labels on a continuum, not categories.

Where the numbers come from

Prices, ratios, the 52-week range and upcoming earnings dates come from Finnhub. Everything on the balance sheet and cash flow statement is pulled straight from the company's own XBRL filings at SEC EDGAR, along with the business description and risk factors quoted from its latest 10-K.

The whole set rebuilds each weekday evening after the American close, so prices are that session's close rather than live. Every card shows how old its data is. Nothing is fetched while you browse: the site is static files, and your cart is yours.

What it cannot know

This is the part worth reading twice. A screen of this kind is blind in specific, knowable ways, and it is more useful to name them than to imply they don't exist.

  • It compares a bank to a grocer. The thresholds are absolute. A supermarket running a 3% net margin is doing fine for a supermarket, and this score marks it down anyway. Until the factors are scored against sector peers rather than fixed numbers, treat cross-industry comparisons as close to meaningless. The card does now set the key figures beside the median for the company's own sector, with where it ranks, so you can make that comparison yourself; the score itself still uses the fixed thresholds.
  • It reads the last filing, not the business. A pending acquisition, a patent cliff, a regulatory inquiry, a founder leaving, a product nobody wants yet: none of that is in the numbers until it lands in a financial statement, by which point the price has usually moved.
  • Accounting is a choice. Two honest companies can report the same economics differently. Write-downs, one-off gains, capitalised costs and unusual fiscal years all distort ratios the score treats as comparable.
  • Missing data is silently forgiven. When a figure is absent the factor is dropped and the rest are rescaled, so a company with thin disclosure can score well on partial evidence. Banks and insurers are the usual case, because their filings do not fit the tags this pipeline reads.
  • Momentum is the past tense. It records what the price has already done. It carries no information about what happens next, and in a downturn it is actively misleading.
  • Nothing here has been backtested. The weights and thresholds are reasoned, not fitted. No claim is made, or should be inferred, that a high score has ever preceded a good return.
  • Parsing fails sometimes. Several hundred filings are read automatically every day. When a figure looks impossible, it probably is. Each card links to the filing it came from, and that filing is the authority, not this page.

What this is not

This is not investment advice, and it is not a recommendation to buy or sell anything. It is a reading of public filings, presented so you can disagree with it.

The pros and cons on each card are plain thresholds, and each one names the figure that triggered it, so you can decide the rule is wrong and still keep the fact underneath. That is the intended use: the score gets you to a shortlist, and then you read the 10-K.
